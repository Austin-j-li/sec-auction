1. Main assessment
Use Mac-Gray A, Providence & Worcester B, and sTec B as the starting ledgers. Mac-Gray D and Providence
& Worcester C are useful cross-checks; sTec C is particularly close to B. These are recommendations about
individual workbooks, not extractors: A–E were randomized independently within each deal and must not be
pooled across deals.

The strongest outputs reconstruct considerably more than a textbook auction. They preserve Mac-Gray's
performance-contingent competing offer, Providence & Worcester's returning bidders and contingent-value
rights, and sTec's preferred bidder withdrawing and returning at a lower price. The principal weaknesses are
usually interpretation and state assignment, not invented quotations or failure to find the main bid prices.

Three problems matter most for structural estimation. First, some ledgers turn an NDA cohort observed over a
period into a precisely dated set of deadline non-submitters. Second, they sometimes fill financing, diligence or
regulatory states from a later passage or a weaker statement than the code requires. Third, economically
important facts can be present in prose but absent from the structured fields that an estimator would
consume. sTec's restriction on allowing rival bids is the clearest example.

None should be ingested directly as estimation-ready data. The leading workbooks are strong
human-reviewable first passes, requiring targeted adjudication rather than wholesale re-extraction. The
weaker workbooks remain useful evidence collections, but their participation and condition fields need more
extensive repair. A genuine source quote and a useful Questions sheet do not make an unsupported
point-coded state safe.

What the scores mean
The scale is 0–100 research usefulness, not a measured percentage of correct cells. I allocated 35 points to
participant identity, entry, selection and exits; 25 to process boundaries, rounds, sequence and deadlines; 30
to bid economics, information and conditions; and 10 to evidence traceability and review usability. A material
error receives more weight than several missing administrative details. I do not reward extra rows merely for
being extra rows, or penalize a justified unknown merely for being unknown.

2. Mac-Gray: a richer offer is not necessarily a better cash bid
The reconstruction against which I assessed the workbooks
The earlier discussions concerned Mac-Gray acquiring CSC; they were not an earlier auction to sell
Mac-Gray. Party A's $17–19 cash indication on 21 June 2013 preceded the broad sale launch on 24 June. The
outreach total was 50 parties, comprising 15 strategic and 35 financial parties. Twenty executed NDAs over
the next two months: two strategic parties and 18 financial parties. Named financial Parties B and C leave a
residual of 16 anonymous financial NDA signers, but that does not establish that all 16 were eligible for the 23
July deadline. [MG pp. 27–33.]

The first deadline produced CSC's $18.50 cash offer on 23 July, B's $17–18 and C's oral $15–17 on 24 July,
and C's written revision to $16–16.50 on 25 July. A, B, C and CSC advanced, with A's NDA executed on 5
August. Financial bidders received broader initial data access than the strategic parties; the latter faced
restrictions on customer-location information. The process also included separate synergy discussions with
strategic bidders. These differences are economically relevant information treatments, not incidental meeting
descriptions. [MG pp. 33–35.]

The next solicitation, issued on 27 August, requested proposals for 9 September. CSC offered $19.50; B
$18.50; A offered $18–19 on 10 September; C offered $16–17 orally that day. The first explicitly final
solicitation began on 11 September with a deadline of 18 September. CSC then offered $20.75 cash. A
reiterated its previous $18–19 indication. B offered $19 cash plus performance options that B valued at $2.50
per share. Its $21.50 headline was neither guaranteed cash nor an ordinary 10% upfront stock component.
[MG pp. 35–37.]

C did not respond to the final solicitation and did not continue. A and B should not automatically be removed
when the committee preferred CSC on 19 September; the executed exclusivity agreement on 24 September
supplies a later closure event. CSC accepted $21.25 on 21 September, with further commitment negotiations
including a $15 million regulatory reverse fee. Full access and confirmatory diligence followed exclusivity.
October negotiations changed sponsor liability, including an unspecified cap in the 5 October draft and a $50
million cap proposed on 8 October. These are material commitment events even without a newly quoted
purchase price. Signing occurred on 14 October; announcement followed on 15 October. [MG pp. 36–41.]

The source does not identify exact individual exit dates for the 16 anonymous NDA signers. The four-party
solicitation on 27 August, after the stated NDA accumulation period, supports an inferred closure by that
stage. That is preferable to a fictional count of 16 eligible bidders all failing the July deadline. It is still a
convention-based upper bound, not a source-reported mass elimination on 27 August. [MG pp. 32–35; v1.14
E3, E8 and E14.]

4. sTec: withdrawal, lower re-entry and restrictions on renewed
competition
The reconstruction against which I assessed the workbooks
The early Company A meeting was cancelled after an NDA draft; the filing does not establish an executed
NDA or live whole-company entry. Company B's early contact similarly did not become an NDA-backed or
bidding entry. An interested asset buyer is not automatically a whole-company competitor. These distinctions
prevent inflation of the auction's starting population. The early cancellation date is not exact enough to
mechanically establish a clean three-month gap before the later approach. [ST pp. 23–26.]

The April process had six named NDA relationships: E, D, F, G, WDC and H. WDC's April 17 addendum reused
an underlying NDA dated January 29, 2009. E and F were reported as interested only in assets later in April.
Under v1.14, that later scope information must not be used to backcast them as partial-only at NDA entry;
their scope change should be recorded. This is a convention for uncertain scope, not proof that they positively
intended a whole-company bid from the start. [ST pp. 26–28; Annex A-3; v1.14 E1 and E3.]

D's April 23 oral indication was strictly greater than $5.60, with additional diligence requests before a written
indication. The May 3 solicitation covered WDC, D and G, not H. WDC submitted $6.60–7.10 cash; D's $5.75
written offer arrived on May 10 after asking for more time. Acceptance of a late offer is evidence about
enforcement even without an expressly announced extension. H's May 15 $5–5.75 proposal was not a late
response to a deadline it had not been given. [ST pp. 28–29.]

The May 16 final-round letters went to WDC and D, requesting responses by May 28. H was not admitted to
that stage but was invited to improve its indication. WDC offered $9.15 on May 28 with a merger markup and
ancillary agreements. D missed deadlines but was allowed to continue; WDC withdrew on May 31, D
continued into June and withdrew on June 5, and WDC returned on June 10 with $6.60–7.10, expressly on its
earlier transaction terms. The final price became $6.85 on June 14. D's continued discussions and WDC's
re-entry need not manufacture a third auction round. [ST pp. 29–32.]

Two later developments are especially important. Cost-Reduction Projections were supplied to WDC and its
adviser on June 19, after the price had been agreed, rather than being the same information given to all earlier
bidders. Separately, WDC resisted waiving the “don't ask, don't waive” restrictions binding E, F and H and
indicated it might not proceed if the target waived them. That was a new material condition affecting the
possibility of renewed competition. It belongs in structured commitment/condition data, not only in a prose
note. [ST pp. 33–34, 45–46.]

A — 89/100: sound participant history, a key condition remains prose-only
A correctly keeps E and F in the entry history before their scope changes, preserves H as a reserve rather than
an admitted finalist, and avoids exiting D merely because it missed a deadline. It has two rounds and correctly
captures WDC's withdrawal and lower return on expressly referenced earlier terms. Its bid rows preserve D's
strict lower threshold, the cash ranges and WDC's document engagement. [ST pp. 26–32; A #22, #28, #34,
#36, #43, #55 and #57.]

The principal repair is #63. It records WDC's restriction on waiving rival standstills as an Other material event,
leaving the structured bid-conditions fields empty. Under E10 and the H3 test, this is a material change in
willingness to proceed and should be represented as a commitment-revision Bid with the condition captured.
The information is not absent from the workbook; it is absent from the typed observations an estimator would
use. Its exact June 20 date is also firmer than the filing's negotiation-window description warrants. [ST pp.
33–34; A #63; v1.14 E10 and E12.]

A does well on continuing diligence and the late projection/clean-team information. It does not recover the
underlying WDC NDA's January 2009 date, which is available in Annex A-3. Overall it is a strong chronology,
but B and C better convert the crucial late competition restriction into research-ready event structure.

5. Where the instructions create or amplify the problem
The tested convention must be distinguished from the economic estimand
I judged whether a workbook followed v1.14, while separately judging whether the resulting representation
serves the research. I did not silently replace it with an older voice-note interpretation. The following
differences affect comparisons even when the source text is read correctly.

“Formal” is a document/process classification, not readiness to close. Earlier collection guidance uses
shortcuts such as treating ranges as informal; the current rule permits a range to be formal when it expressly
retains definitive-document terms. sTec's June 10 $6.60–7.10 return is the natural example. Conversely, PW's
July 26 price-only revision can be Informal under the communication-specific rule despite occurring after a
formal July 21 proposal. An estimator must not interpret such codes as a bidder losing all earlier knowledge or
commitment. Preserve the formal route, document engagement, conditions and stage separately. [Collection
Instructions p. 7; v1.14 E10–E12; PW pp. 29–30; ST p. 32.]

Material commitment changes are not necessarily new price observations. The current instruction correctly
asks for bidder liability or other commitment revisions as Bid events. It also restricts price carry-forward. This
produces perfectly legitimate Bid rows with blank prices. Counting every Bid row as an independent new
valuation observation would overcount Mac-Gray's October liability negotiations and sTec's standstill
restriction. The instruction would benefit from one small explicit distinction between a new/revised price and
a non-price commitment revision, rather than requiring downstream users to infer it from blanks and prose.
[v1.14 E10–E11; MG p. 39; ST pp. 33–34.]

Communication-specific state rules are difficult to combine with information accumulated over time. The
instruction appropriately prohibits projecting signing certainty backward. But “Not stated at this
communication” and “not known to the researcher at this date” are not the same. The first may be appropriate
in a bid row while a separately supported diligence-history state is Incomplete. This explains some defensible
A/D versus B/C differences in sTec. A minimal improvement is to state whether each field describes the
current communication, the known state at that time, or an explicit bid condition. It should not invite
